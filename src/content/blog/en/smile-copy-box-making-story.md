## Getting Started

At the time, I was using a reward-based app (commonly known as "app-tech" in Korea). where you solve quizzes generated by AI to earn a little pocket money. The quizzes included a feature that let you ask the AI for hints, and after using it for a while, I realized the quality of the hints varied dramatically depending on how the prompt was written.

So I started creating multiple versions of prompts to get better hints.

The problem was that constantly saving prompts in a notes app, copying them, and pasting them over and over became surprisingly tedious. That was when I came up with the idea for a clipboard service dedicated to prompts.

To be honest, there was no real need for me to build one myself. Plenty of clipboard services that already met my standards existed on the market. But, much like with Streak Dial, I liked that it was an idea simple enough to build lightly, without complicated features, so I decided to make it myself.
&nbsp;

&nbsp; 
## Familiar, But Not Too Familiar

The idea itself was fairly ordinary, but I didn’t want to make just another ordinary clipboard app. So I added one extra element: something meant to “lift your mood.”

Nothing grand — just the idea of displaying a smiley face on the screen to brighten the user's mood.

I don’t remember where I heard it exactly, but I once came across the idea that simply seeing a smiling face icon tends to make the brain process it as a “positive social signal.” I thought that if I applied this idea to a service, it might at least give users a small emotional hook.
&nbsp;

&nbsp; 
## Intentional Friction

The app itself is simple. You add a sentence, and tapping it instantly copies it to the clipboard. It’s useful for storing phrases you use occasionally or frequently, like your home address or common greetings.

Because the structure was so simple, the UX could have been extremely streamlined as well. But I intentionally made parts of the UX less convenient. To add new text or edit the list, users must first tap the smiley face on the screen to unlock these actions.

I felt that deliberately increasing interactions with the smiley face was the best way to emphasize the app’s defining characteristic.

## Design

I wanted the smiley face to feel a little alive, so I created a Lottie animation that makes it gently nod and sway. I also added small details to reduce the flatness of the app, such as scrolling text at the top of the screen and a gradient background that gradually becomes redder toward the bottom.

## Closing Thoughts

Since I’m writing this long after finishing the app, I can now see plenty of areas that feel lacking.

At the same time, I also think it’s more usable than I expected. (Though that may simply be because the app barely has any features.)

The number of downloads since launch has been modest, and the growth has been very slow. Even without any promotion, I still find it fascinating that people download the app at all, but the results are admittedly far below what I had hoped for.

Since release, I’ve shipped four updates. Aside from a font issue where certain characters didn’t display correctly, the updates were all under-the-hood tweaks. I briefly considered rebuilding the app from the ground up, but eventually decided it would be better to spend that time focusing on other projects instead.

Hopefully, someday, I’ll come back to it again.
